Noble Skywave Radio Desk

Creator · 2026 · 6 weeks · 1 person · 11 min read

A browser-based HF radio desk I built for Exercise Noble Skywave 2026. It takes a radio check from a Slack message to a scored contact - agreeing the exact dial, tuning a FlexRadio, making one guarded voice call, recording and transcribing the reply, and preparing the five-minute Web App report - alongside a public map of all 430 registered stations.

Overview

Exercise Noble Skywave is an international HF skywave competition run by the Canadian Forces School of Communications and Electronics. Over two days, more than 200 teams worldwide arrange radio checks in a shared Slack workspace, try to make each contact on air, and score only when both teams log the same contact on the exercise Web App within five minutes. I built the station software for NZ1, a New Zealand Army team at Trentham: a browser console and an automated desk that read and answered Slack, tuned a FlexRadio FLEX-8600 to the agreed dial, made voice calls with text-to-speech, recorded and transcribed what came back, and kept the evidence for every step. Alongside it I published a free station map so every team could see where the others were, and how far away.

Problem

From New Zealand nearly every other team is 10,000 to 18,000 km away, and much of the competition runs through the local night. A single scored contact needs a Slack conversation to agree a frequency and who calls first, someone at the radio at the moment the other team is ready, a call and a reply that both sides actually hear, and matching Web App entries from both teams within five minutes. By hand, most of the effort goes into bookkeeping: which of a dozen conversations is on which dial, who is meant to transmit, what was really heard, and whether the other team has logged yet. Offers go stale within minutes as teams change frequency or go off shift, and a contact the other side forgets to log scores nothing.

Constraints

  • The desk could key a real HF transmitter, so every call had to stay inside an operator-approved scope: authorised dials and modes, per-station and total call budgets, EMCON, SWR and power interlocks, and a hard key-time limit
  • One radio handled both directions, so a receive window, an operator listening by hand and an automated call could never be allowed to fight over it
  • Scoring needs both teams to log within five minutes of the contact ending, so reporting had to be fast and the evidence unambiguous
  • Coordination happened in a busy shared Slack with many conversations at once, and replies had to read like a person on a radio desk, not a bot
  • Receive at the site was noisy, so silence could never be treated as proof that a station wasn't there

Approach

The app is a FastAPI service with a React console - the Soft KDU - on top of SQLite. Slack traffic, radio state, recordings, transcripts and reports all go through one API, and the console and the automated desk both use it, so the operator sees exactly what the automation sees and can take over any conversation at any moment. The desk is a loop around that API. It picks up new requests from the main channel and direct messages, agrees an exact dial and who calls first, checks the propagation forecast for the path before agreeing, claims the radio, verifies the tune, antenna and power, makes one bounded text-to-speech call, opens a receive window, transcribes the reply with faster-whisper, asks the other team for a 1-5 report, and drafts the Web App entry with a visible five-minute countdown. Conversational replies are written by an LLM that runs with no tools; everything that touches the radio is deterministic code behind the same safety gates. Much of the code was written with AI coding agents (Claude Code and OpenAI Codex) working against the test suite, with me deciding what went anywhere near the transmitter.

Key Decisions

Keep a human approval gate and hard RF interlocks around every transmission, even after transmit was authorised

Reasoning:

Once the station was cleared to transmit, the tempting next step was to let the desk call whenever it liked. Instead every call runs inside a written scope the operator approves: the dials and mode it may use, a call budget per station and in total, EMCON, SWR and power limits, a key-time cap, and a manual takeover that stops the automation at once. The interlocks earned their place during the exercise: a mismatched antenna tripped the SWR abort on three frequencies and an over-driven power setting tripped the watchdog, and each time the call stopped within about a second rather than pushing power into a bad match.

Alternatives considered:
  • Fully unattended transmit once the station was authorised
  • The operator keying every call by hand, which is safe but too slow to keep up through the night

Record each direction of a radio check separately, with its own evidence

Reasoning:

A radio check has two halves that fail independently: did we hear them, and did they hear us. The contact log keeps them apart - our recording and transcript for the return, the other team's own report for the outbound call - and only both together become two-way. "Ready", "listening" or a queued invitation never count as reception, and a transcript the speech model wasn't sure of stays unresolved instead of becoming a success. That kept the record honest on a lopsided circuit: stations in Peru and Japan copied NZ1 when NZ1 couldn't copy them.

Alternatives considered:
  • A single pass/fail result per check
  • Treating any transcript that contains a callsign as a confirmed contact

Serialise every RF action through one pipeline, across processes

Reasoning:

With several conversations live at once, the hard part wasn't making a call, it was guaranteeing that nothing else used the radio while it happened. A file lock serialises call preparation and keying across processes, the radio is claimed for each receive or transmit window, and only one transmit offer can be live at a time. Each rule came from a real failure: an automated check once retuned the radio in the middle of a hand-listen, and a restarted desk overlapped a manual call and interrupted it.

Alternatives considered:
  • One worker per conversation, each driving the radio directly
  • A simple global queue without claims, which can't protect an operator listening by hand

Check the propagation forecast before agreeing a frequency

Reasoning:

Teams often proposed dials that could not work over 12,000 km or more at that hour. Before agreeing, the desk looks up the other station's position, asks the GRAFEX forecast for the path, and if the dial is below the usable window it offers the best dial from the chart in the same reply. One move message replaced an agree-then-refuse exchange, and repeat suggestions are suppressed so a station isn't asked to move twice.

Alternatives considered:
  • Accept any proposed dial and let the call fail
  • Leave every frequency choice to the operator

Publish the station roster as its own public site

Reasoning:

Every team needed the same answers - where is this station, what grid square, how far, which bearing - and the roster only existed as a 430-row spreadsheet. I published that part on its own as a static site with no radio controls, logs or coordination data. Shared corrections are submitted as GitHub issues and applied by a reviewed workflow that validates each change before publishing, so nobody can edit the shared roster anonymously.

Alternatives considered:
  • Keep the map inside the private console
  • A shared, editable spreadsheet

Tech Stack

  • Python 3 + FastAPI (REST and WebSocket)
  • React + TypeScript + Vite
  • FlexRadio SmartSDR API (FLEX-8600 + Maestro)
  • faster-whisper speech-to-text
  • Piper text-to-speech
  • SQLite
  • KiwiSDR and HackRF One receive sources
  • GRAFEX and VOACAP propagation planning
  • Slack API
  • Playwright
  • Leaflet maps
  • Claude for conversational replies (no tools)
  • Claude Code and OpenAI Codex as coding agents

Result & Impact

  • 38 voice calls and 304 receive checks with 92 stations over the two-day competition
    Desk activity
  • 8,147 recordings (27 hours of audio) and 6,866 transcripts, each tied to its dial, antenna and time
    Evidence kept
  • Two-way voice with Portugal at 18,114 km; heard in Peru and Japan from about 40 W
    Longest contact
  • None - every call stayed inside its approved budget and interlocks
    Unsafe transmissions

The desk did the job it was built for: it kept dozens of conversations straight, made calls safely through the New Zealand night, and left an evidence trail good enough to write the after-action report from primary records. NZ1 finished with 110.5 points across three of the four stages. It also made the real bottleneck obvious. From Trentham, NZ1 was heard far more often than it could hear, so the next gain isn't more automation - it's a quieter receive antenna and a second receiver.

Learnings

  • A queue position is not a radio call. The first coordination panel labelled stations "Calling" when they had only been invited; it now shows evidence-based stages and only says Transmitting when the radio itself reports it
  • Fail loudly. Late in the exercise the receive audio stream stalled. The desk correctly refused to report stations as "not heard", but nothing alerted the operator overnight, so the last calls went out with nothing listening for the reply
  • Hand-offs have to work in both directions. An operator reply once paused a conversation indefinitely and stations waited for answers that never came; hand-off pauses now lapse after five minutes of operator silence
  • Ask people for the one thing the radio can't tell you. A short "reply 1-5 with your callsign" prompt after each call turned vague chat into a usable report
  • Most of the engineering went into coordination and evidence, not the radio itself

The app screenshots below are the real interface, rendered offline with simulated stations (US901, AU901 and friends) for the workflow showcase. No live traffic, real stations or radio were involved in making them.

Agreeing the check

Every contact starts as a message. The desk matches the sender to the station roster, agrees one exact dial and mode and who will call first, and waits for a fresh “listening” before it touches the radio. Replies typed in the console go through the same outbox as automatic ones, so they’re de-duplicated, retried and shown with their delivery state. Any conversation can be taken over by hand, and the automation stays out of it until it’s handed back.

One guarded call

When the other station is listening, the desk claims the radio, tunes and verifies the dial, checks the antenna and tuner, and makes a single text-to-speech call of about twenty seconds. Authorisation, readiness, RF condition and stop conditions all have to pass first, and EMCON or a manual takeover stops everything. A transmission is only evidence of an attempt: it becomes a successful outbound check when the other team reports hearing NZ1.

Listening, and keeping the original

The reply window records the receiver audio with its dial, mode and antenna, and faster-whisper transcribes it, primed with the callsigns expected on that check. The original audio is always kept beside the transcript, so an operator can listen again, correct the text, or leave it unresolved when the evidence is weak. A HackRF One and public KiwiSDR receivers can feed the same pipeline as extra ears.

Two directions, reported separately

The contact log answers two questions separately - did NZ1 hear them, and did they hear NZ1 - and only both together make a two-way contact. When a contact is confirmed, a draft for the exercise Web App appears with a five-minute countdown, because both teams have to log within five minutes for it to score.

The whole desk on a map

The circuit map shows what the desk is doing right now: blue for coordination, orange for an outbound call and teal for a receive window - and only when the radio’s own telemetry backs it up. A queue position never draws a transmit path.

The fieldbook: a public station map

The part every team could use is public. The Noble Skywave station map puts all 430 registered stations on a map and in a sortable table, with 4- and 6-character grid squares and great-circle distance and bearing from any station you choose. Pick NZ1 as the reference and select PT1, and it draws the roughly 18,100 km path to Portugal - NZ1’s longest two-way contact of the exercise. It runs entirely in the browser with no radio controls, logs or analytics, and shared corrections go through GitHub issues and a reviewed publishing workflow.

Where it stands

Noble Skywave 2026 finished on 22 October. NZ1 scored 110.5 points, was heard in Peru and Japan from about 40 W, and worked Portugal two-way by voice. The desk ran safely within its limits throughout, and the record it kept made the after-action review straightforward. Before the next exercise the priorities are on the receive side: a quieter receive antenna, a second receiver so one radio isn’t doing both jobs, and an alarm the moment receive audio stops.
